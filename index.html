Does training improve satisfaction?
How would you analyse this?

  • AAverage the score in each training group and compare means
  • BRegress the score on hours of training (a straight line)
  • CSplit into “satisfied / not satisfied” and run logistic regression
Each of these throws something away. Let's see what, and then fix it.
Ask the room. Most hands go to B (treat the 1–5 score as a number) or C (dichotomise). A treats 1–5 as interval too. We are going to try B and C on the actual data and watch what goes wrong, then build the ordinal model from the pieces.

The line assumes every step is one unit wide — the equal-spacing assumption we just said we can't make.
Points pop in by category colour. The OLS line draws itself; the parts above 5 and below 1 are highlighted. Two problems: impossible predictions and the hidden equal-spacing assumption. A third (not shown) is heteroskedastic, non-normal residuals by construction.
Attempt C · satisfied or not

Collapse to two groups, fit a curve

Call 4–5 satisfied, 1–3 not. Now the outcome is 0/1 and a logistic curve fits.

Fitted: α, β. Each extra hour multiplies the odds of being satisfied by –.

But 5 categories became 2. “Very dissatisfied” and “Neutral” are now the same thing. We threw away the order.
Points recolour into two groups and drop to 0/1; the sigmoid traces in. The equation is built one term at a time: logit, the probability, equals, α, plus βx. This is ordinary binary logistic regression — good, but it discarded three of the four boundaries between categories.
The fix · keep every boundary

Why choose one cut? Use all four.

Four ways to split 1–5: 1 | 2–5, 1–2 | 3–5, 1–3 | 4–5, 1–4 | 5. Fit a curve to each.

They come out almost parallel. So assume they are: one slope, four intercepts.

The proportional-odds (cumulative logit) model: cut-points τj, one shared slope β.
Four separately fitted curves trace in one at a time. Then, on the next step, they snap to the constrained parallel fit. Watch the equation: P(Y ≥ 4) becomes P(Y > j), and α is covered by −τ_j. That single morph is the whole model. Software conventions differ: R's polr writes logit P(Y ≤ j) = τ_j − βx; Stata's ologit reports the same τ as "cut points". Same model, sign flipped.

A hidden continuous scale

Imagine each employee has an unobserved satisfaction propensity Y*. Training shifts it right by β per hour; noise ε spreads it out.

We only see which interval it lands in. The cut-points τ1…τ4 are fixed; the curve slides. Drag the slider.

Was “parallel” a fair assumption?

Check it, don't hope

Brant's idea: fit each cut separately and ask whether the four βs agree. Here the common slope sits inside every interval.

If the test rejects: free the slope only for the offending predictor — a partial proportional-odds model (Stata gologit2, R VGAM / ordinal::clm).
Toggle to let the four curves take their own slopes — the fan is what a violated assumption would look like. The dot-and-whisker chart shows each cut's separate slope with 95% CI against the shared slope. Software: Brant test (Stata brant, R brant), or the likelihood-ratio test against a generalised ordered logit.
Where you will meet this in management & IS research

Anything ranked, staged, graded or rated

Technology adoption stage

Not considering → evaluating → piloting → scaled. Predictors: firm size, IT budget, top-management support.

A single Likert item

“I intend to use the system” (1–7) from a TAM / UTAUT survey. An item is ordinal; a summed scale is usually treated as continuous.

IT project outcome

Failed / challenged / successful by size, agile vs waterfall, executive sponsor. Sponsor may matter more at the “failed” cut — a partial-PO case.

Customer satisfaction · NPS

Detractor / passive / promoter by wait time, channel, first-contact resolution. Cumulative odds ratios are what marketing can act on.

Maturity level

CMMI-style level 1–5, digital-maturity tiers, supplier risk tiers, credit grades. Ordinal by construction — never interval.

Employees nested in teams

Burnout severity or turnover intention with people inside teams inside firms → add random intercepts: a cumulative link mixed model.

Six vignettes. Each maps to a feature of the model: mixed predictors, item-vs-scale, partial proportional odds, business interpretation, ordinal-by-construction, and nesting.
Before you fit · the data structure you need

One row per unit, one ordered outcome

  • Outcome: a single column with K ≥ 3 ordered levels, stored as an ordered factor, not a number. Every level needs cases.
  • Predictors: any mix — continuous, 0/1 dummies, nominal (dummy-coded), or ordinal (decide: numeric, dummies, or monotone).
  • Sample size: roughly 10–15 cases per parameter; watch the smallest outcome category, it sets the limit.
  • Nesting? Respondents in teams / firms → add a grouping column and fit a mixed model.
  • Item or scale? One Likert item → ordinal regression. A multi-item summed scale → usually OLS / SEM.

Recap

Ordinal regression in one breath

An ordinal outcome is a ranking with unknown gaps. A line pretends the gaps are equal; a 0/1 split throws the ranking away.

The proportional-odds model is K − 1 logistic regressions that share one slope and differ only in their cut-points.

eβ is the odds of being higher on the scale, per unit of x, the same at every cut. Test that it is.

Behind it sits a latent continuous Y* carved by thresholds τ — the picture with the sliding bell.
