Why the log?

p0.45
Target 2:00. Odds, log-odds and the inverse (logistic function) with the Pew numbers. Drag the slider (try 0.2 then 0.8 to show symmetry; push to the ends to show the range). Why the log, three reasons: (1) range: probability is bounded, log-odds are not, so any linear predictor gives a valid probability; (2) symmetry: 0.2 and 0.8 have log-odds −1.39 and +1.39, while their odds (0.25 vs 4) are lopsided; (3) multiplicative → additive: a predictor multiplies the odds, the log makes that an added β, and e^β is the odds ratio. If asked for the formal reason: the logit is the canonical link for binomial data in generalised linear models, and it is exactly what you get when the hidden-scale errors follow a logistic distribution (slide 13). Why the log (say it, it is no longer on the slide): (1) Any number is allowed: a regression xβ can be any value; on the log-odds scale every value maps back to a valid probability. (2) Symmetry: 0.2 and 0.8 are mirror images, odds 0.25 vs 4 but log-odds −1.39 vs +1.39; reversing the scale just flips the sign. (3) Multiplying becomes adding: effects multiply the odds, and the log turns that into adding β, so e^β is an odds ratio, the key to "proportional odds".

If asked how the model gives probabilities: put θ and β back into the logistic formula, P(Y ≤ j) = e^(θ−βx) / (1 + e^(θ−βx)), then subtract neighbouring cuts. SPSS does this for us. The proportional odds assumption: test of parallel lines

Ordinal logistic regression assumes one β for every cut: a predictor has the same effect wherever we split the scale (slide 11), so the lines are parallel. We check this with the test of parallel lines.
H0: the slopes do not differ
p-value large (fail to reject H0)
So proportional odds is reasonable: we can use the ordinal model
H1: the slopes do differ
p-value small (reject H0)
So proportional odds is not reasonable: the predictor's effect changes from cut to cut
Other assumptions, as in most regressions
Ordered outcome
Independent observations
No severe multicollinearity
Enough cases in each answer
Target 1:30. Click 1: the assumption in one sentence: one β at every cut, i.e. parallel lines (point back to slide 11, the ratio about 1.4 at every cut, and slide 12, the same 0.335 in every row). Click 2: H0, slopes do not differ; large p → OK to use the model. Click 3: H1, slopes differ; small p → the assumption fails; options are a partial proportional-odds model (what we do in Part 2) or multinomial logit. In SPSS: Output → tick "Test of parallel lines". Click 4: the other assumptions are the usual ones, just name them. If asked: the test is sensitive to sample size. With thousands of respondents (Pew, n = 4,872) even small differences are "significant"; with a hundred (Choi et al., n = 109) real differences can be missed. So also look at how different the per-cut slopes actually are. If asked what to do when the test fails: let only the problem predictors have a different slope at each cut (partial proportional odds model), or drop the order and use multinomial logit.

Why not OLS or multinomial logit?

OLS regression
treats 1–5 as numbers
12345

Assumes equal gaps: "Not too" to "Somewhat" is the same distance as "Very" to "Extremely".

Multinomial logit
treats answers as unordered labels
31524

Ignores the order: "Extremely" is just another category, like a colour.

Ordinal logistic
keeps the order
12345

Uses the order, no equal gaps: the cut-offs θ are estimated from the data.

Target 1:00. Click 1: OLS treats the answers as numbers 1 to 5, so it assumes the gaps between answers are equal; nothing in the question tells us that. Click 2: multinomial logit treats the answers as unordered labels and throws the order away (and needs a separate set of coefficients for each answer). Click 3: ordinal logistic keeps the order but lets the data decide where the cut-offs are (the θs), with one β per predictor; the price is the proportional-odds assumption from the previous slide. If asked about splitting into yes/no (binary logit): it throws away categories and the result depends on where you cut. Extra points per card (no longer on the slide): OLS can predict impossible values (e.g. 5.6). Multinomial logit needs many more parameters (one set per answer). Ordinal logistic has one β per predictor but needs proportional odds (previous slide).

It's the same result as the odds ratio, just in a form that's easier to understand." Right model? Assumptions met?

✓ The right family of model

The outcome is ordered (0–4) with unknown gaps, so an ordinal model beats OLS or a yes/no split.

✗ Is a count one ordered scale?

Marking a post as spam and complaining in public both score 1 point, as if they were the same.

✗ Parallel lines "met", but unchecked

No test statistic reported, and with only 109 students a non-significant test proves little.

✗ Too few cases at the top

Mostly zeros, empty top categories, and odds ratios of 18 and 25: a warning sign.

Target 2:00. One sentence per box. Box 1: credit first. The outcome is ordered and we don't know the gaps between 1, 2, 3 and 4 behaviours, so ordinal logistic is a sensible choice and better than OLS on a 0–4 score with this many zeros. Box 2: the outcome is a count of different behaviours. Is 2 really "more avoidance" than 1 if the behaviours are very different? Box 3: the paper says "we conducted parallel lines tests … and concluded that the proportional odds assumption was met" (p. 688, footnote 6), but gives no χ², df or p. With 109 people the test has little power: not significant does not mean the assumption holds (compare Pew, where 4,872 people led to rejection). Credit: at least they tested it, which many papers skip. Box 4: 68–77% of students did nothing, nobody scored 4, and approach never went above 2. With so few non-zero cases, odds ratios of about 18 and 25 per standard deviation are suspiciously large (a sign of near-separation). If asked: they also treated the three outcomes as independent, though 37 inactive students are zero on all three. Alternatives: a binary model per outcome, a hurdle/count model, or a joint model.

✓ Clear, comparable effects

Predictors standardised; directions read correctly.

✗ Pseudo-R² misread

"Correctly predicts about 33%": pseudo-R² is not % correct.

✗ Only β and p reported

No odds ratios, confidence intervals or predicted probabilities.

✗ Feelings were measured, not manipulated

This step is correlational, not experimental.

Target 1:30. Box 1: credit. They standardised the two feelings, so the effects are comparable, read the signs correctly, and compared each model with a covariates-only model. Box 2: the clear mistake. They write "Model A correctly predicts about 33% of inaction" (p. 688), but that number is a Cox–Snell pseudo-R². Pseudo-R² is not the share of cases predicted correctly (we saw this on the Part 2 fit slide), and Cox–Snell cannot even reach 1. Box 3: reporting is thin: coefficients and p-values only (full tables only in the online appendix). No thresholds, odds ratios, CIs or predicted probabilities, so readers can't judge how big the effects are. Box 4: the experiment manipulated tagging and shared friends, but the ordinal models use the measured feelings as predictors, so this stage shows association, not cause. Mediation was tested with the older Baron and Kenny steps.

How did the method contribute to knowledge?

✓ It made the main finding possible

Counting how many behaviours, not just whether, revealed two levels of avoidance with different drivers.

Verdict

The right model, used with too little data and too little reporting. Trust the directions of the effects more than their sizes.

Target 1:00. Box 1: the ordinal outcome is what let them separate avoiding the post from avoiding the friend, the paper's distinctive contribution. A yes/no outcome would have hidden how strongly people reacted. Box 2: balanced verdict. Right family of model, but small sample, sparse categories and thin reporting, so believe the directions, be careful with the magnitudes (odds ratios of 18 and 25). If asked what they could have done better: report the parallel-lines test (χ², df, p); report odds ratios with CIs and predicted probabilities; check sparse categories and try a simpler binary or hurdle model as a robustness check.

Takeaways

1Use ordinal logistic regression for ordered answers, such as ratings, satisfaction or agreement scales.
2An ordinal outcome is a ranking with unknown gaps: the order matters, but the distances between answers are not necessarily equal (e.g. the gap from "not too" to "somewhat" may differ from the gap from "very" to "extremely").
3The proportional odds model is K − 1 logistic regressions sharing one slope. eβ is the cumulative odds ratio: how much the odds of a higher answer change, the same at every cut.
4Test proportional odds with the test of parallel lines.
